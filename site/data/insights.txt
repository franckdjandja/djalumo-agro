Petrol pump or solar pump? For an irrigated project, the answer is not in the purchase price but in the operating account.

In Cameroon, a petrol motor pump costs about 200,000 FCFA. Its real cost comes later. On one hectare of open-field tomato planted in December, the Season Planner counts some 290 litres of petrol for the dry season, about 245,000 FCFA at 840 FCFA per litre. Over three dry seasons, that is about 735,000 FCFA, or 1,260 USD, per hectare. At the same rate, a 100-hectare project would burn about 126,000 USD of fuel.

Solar turns that recurring bill into an upfront investment. Entry-level solar pumps started at about 600 USD in 2021, and Efficiency for Access estimated that once fuel is counted, they pay for themselves in under 18 months. At commercial scale, the system is sized to the area, the depth of the water and the hours of sunshine.

The same arithmetic holds across sub-Saharan Africa, where solar irrigation is studied in the field by the International Water Management Institute (IWMI).

The useful question is which energy cost the project carries over its life, per hectare and per season. The Season Planner prices the pump, its fuel and drip irrigation for every planting month.

The dry season has begun in West Cameroon. From November to February, Foumbot receives on average about 78 mm of rain in total, while evaporative demand reaches about 556 mm over the same four months.

Almost all the water a crop needs from now until March will have to come from irrigation. Of those 78 mm, only about 27 are dependable enough to count on, and nearly all of them fall in November. December and January bring 3 to 4 mm each.

Heat adds to the pressure. The average maximum temperature climbs from 29 °C in December to 32 °C in February, the hottest month of the year. That is the threshold the Season Planner uses for tomato fruit set; sweet pepper already suffers from 30 °C.

Three decisions follow for any dry-season investment. Secure the water source and its cost before buying seed. Choose the planting date so that flowering avoids the February heat. Budget irrigation as a line of its own: equipment, energy and labour. They apply at every scale, from the Cameroon highlands to the Sahel.

The rains usually return in March and settle in April. The Season Planner scores every planting month for water and heat.

These four photographs follow one cycle. A nursery under insect netting. Young plants staked one by one after transplanting. Rows weeded in May. Plants at fruit set.

Staking is quiet work, but it shapes the harvest. A staked plant keeps its fruit clean and ventilated, which limits rot and speeds up picking. The norm the Season Planner applies, from Cameroon's Ministry of Agriculture, is one stake per plant: about 25,000 per hectare, 2 m tall for indeterminate varieties, tied in two passes.

At project scale it becomes a supply question. One hundred hectares need about 2.5 million stakes, cut, transported and renewed. No reliable market price is published for them, so the Planner keeps the quantity in every plan and flags a local quote before any commitment.

On this site, quality followed. The margin did not: whiteflies, caterpillars and fruit rot had to be fought all season, and local prices stayed low. Staking protects the crop; only a pest calendar and a sales plan protect the result.
